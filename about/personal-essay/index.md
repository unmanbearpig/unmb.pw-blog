# I love and hate computers enough to keep asking how they really work.

I wrote a version of that sentence on this site years ago, when the About page was mostly a few lines about Rails, Rust, security and privacy. It still describes me better than a list of technologies. Computers are frustrating because they turn human ambiguity into very literal failure. They are satisfying for exactly the same reason: if you stay with a problem, trace the system and test your assumptions, the vague thing eventually becomes concrete.

I started professional software work in 2009 on testing infrastructure for Fibre Channel switches. Since then I have worked on search-engine acquisition, marketplaces, education software, regulatory extraction, speech data, infrastructure and my own products. Ruby on Rails and PostgreSQL became my commercial base because they are unusually good tools for turning a changing business into maintainable software. Go and Python now carry much of my product and data work. Rust, C and lower-level Linux work remain useful whenever I need to understand what the abstractions are hiding.

The job title matters less to me than the shape of the responsibility. I do my best work when I can follow a problem across boundaries. A slow page may be a query, a cache policy, a product assumption or all three. A website request may really be an inventory workflow. An AI result is not useful because a model produced plausible text; it is useful when the input, evaluation, provenance and failure behavior are good enough for someone to make a decision.

HostelPunk is the clearest current example. I wanted to find hostels where people actually meet each other, and generic platform scores were not answering that question. The result became a public system spanning data acquisition, entity resolution, multilingual review analysis, ranking, warnings, interface and operations. It now processes 74 million reviews, links more than 22,000 properties and maintains 235 versioned signals. The product is deliberately honest about confidence and evidence because narrow, inspectable claims are more useful than one magical score.

Vestuario Antiguo exercises a different part of the same instinct. Tatiana came with a real business, an initial direction and references. I turned that into a complete visual identity and logo, a public brand experience and a Rails system for the operational rules behind a rental atelier. I continue to manage the technical product. It is satisfying work because visual decisions, customer promises and staff workflows all have to agree.

Coding agents have changed how quickly I can move, but not what I consider engineering. I use them extensively. I also define the work, choose the architecture, inspect the output, write or demand the relevant tests, deploy it and remain responsible when production behaves differently from the happy path. Speed is valuable when someone still knows where to apply skepticism.

Outside paid work, I tend to build tools around whatever I am curious about. That has produced a music-listening statistics service, a Swift and Rust launcher, a Firefox extension, a Linux kernel patch, photo and travel notes, and investigations into how technology affects the people using it. I am self-taught, which probably explains both the range and my preference for learning from the mechanism rather than the label.

I live in Santiago, Chile. English is my working language; my Spanish is still basic. I am looking for senior backend or product-engineering work where long production memory, direct communication and ownership are more useful than a narrow technology identity.
